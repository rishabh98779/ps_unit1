Unit 1 · 1.1 Introduction of Basic Statistics

Lecture 1.1 · SOURCE CONTENT

1. Formal Definitions

Formal Definition — Probability Theory

Probability theory is the study of the mathematical rules that govern random events.

Formal Definition — Statistics

Statistics is the application of probability to the collection, analysis, and description of random data.

Formal Definition — Random Event (informal)

A random event is an event in which we do not know the outcome without observing it. Probability tells us what we can say about such events, given our assumptions about the possible outcomes.

Hinglish Explanation: Probability ka kaam hai randomness ko mathematical rules mein capture karna — jaise coin toss ka outcome pehle se pakka nahi hota, par uske chances quantify kar sakte hain. Statistics probability ko real data pe apply karta hai: experiments design karna, data summarize karna, aur duniya ke baare mein conclusions nikalna. Simple words mein — Probability theory banati hai, Statistics usse data pe lagata hai.

2. What Statistics Is Used For

  • Design experiments
  • Summarize data
  • Make conclusions about the world
  • Explore complex data

3. Population vs Sample

Formal Definition — Population & Sample

A population is the entire collection of objects under study (e.g. all games played by Babe Ruth). A sample is a subset of the population (e.g. a random sample of 20 games). Measures computed from the entire population are population parameters; from a sample they are sample statistics (sample mean, sample median, sample mode).

Hinglish: Population matlab poori collection — har member ke paas variable ki ek value hoti hai (yahi data hai). Agar poore population pe mean/median/mode nikalein toh population mean etc. milta hai; sample se nikala toh sample mean — jo population ka estimate hota hai. Exam mein "population vs sample" ka distinction frequently poocha jata hai.

Unit 1 · 1.2 Measures of Central Tendency

Lecture 1.2 · Mean, Median, Mode SOURCE CONTENT

1. Big Picture (Hinglish)

Concept: Jab do ya zyada datasets compare karne hote hain, toh sirf frequency distribution ya graphs se baat nahi banti — data ko ek single value mein summarize karna padta hai. Ye value center ke aas-paas hoti hai, aur poore dataset ko represent karti hai — isliye isse measure of central tendency (ya average) kehte hain. Kyunki ye value distribution ki X-axis pe position batati hai, isse measure of location/position bhi kehte hain. Types: Arithmetic Mean, Geometric Mean, Harmonic Mean, Median, Mode — par hamare course mein main focus Mean, Median, Mode pe hai.

2. Arithmetic Mean

Formal Definition — Arithmetic Mean

Arithmetic Mean is the value obtained by dividing the sum of all the observations by the number of observations: \(\bar{x} = \frac{\sum x_i}{n}\) (ungrouped), \(\bar{x} = \frac{\sum f_i x_i}{\sum f_i}\) (grouped).

Three Methods for Grouped Data

Direct Method: \(\bar{x} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} f_i x_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} f_i}\), n = \(\sum f_i\)
Short-cut (Assumed Mean) Method: \(\bar{x} = A + \frac{\sum f_i D_i}{\sum f_i}\), where \(D_i = x_i - A\), A = assumed mean
Step-Deviation Method: \(\bar{x} = A + \frac{\sum f_i u_i}{\sum f_i} \times h\), where \(u_i = \frac{x_i - A}{h}\), h = class width
Method Selection (Hinglish): Direct method sabse straightforward hai — midpoint × frequency karke divide karo. Agar numbers bade hain, toh short-cut method se ek assumed mean A ghata ke calculations aasaan ho jaati hain. Agar class intervals equal width ke hain, toh step-deviation best hai kyunki deviations h se divide karke chhote integers ban jaate hain. Exam mein agar "step deviation method" bola gaya hai, toh wahi use karna mandatory hai.
Example 1 — Mean (Ungrouped, Basic) SOURCE

Problem: Marks of 9 students: 45, 32, 37, 46, 39, 36, 41, 48, 36. Find mean.

Sum: 45+32+37+46+39+36+41+48+36 = 360; n = 9
Mean \(\bar{x} = 360/9 = 40\)
Final Answer: Mean = 40 marks.

Properties of Arithmetic Mean (Exam favorites)

  • The mean of a constant is that constant itself.
  • The sum of deviations from the mean is zero: \(\sum (x_i - \bar{x}) = 0\).
  • The sum of squared deviations is minimized at the mean: \(\sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2 \le \sum(x_i-A)^2\) for any A.
  • Arithmetic mean is affected by change of origin and scale (adding/subtracting/multiplying/dividing every value by a constant changes the mean accordingly).
  • Given group means and sizes, the composite (combined) mean can be found algebraically.

Advantages

  • Readily understood; easy computation; algebraic treatment possible (combined mean).
  • Can be computed even if individual observations are unknown — only their sum and count needed.
  • No need to arrange data (unlike median) or form distribution (unlike mode).
  • Stable against sampling fluctuations — most reliable average.

Disadvantages

  • Cannot be obtained by inspection (unlike median/mode).
  • Badly affected by extreme values; cannot be computed from open-end classes.
  • May not be an actual value of the variable (e.g. 2.54 children per family).

3. Median

Formal Definition — Median

Median is the value of the middle-most observation when the data is arranged in ascending (or descending) order. For odd n it is the \(\frac{n+1}{2}\)th observation; for even n it is the mean of the \(\frac{n}{2}\)th and \(( \frac{n}{2}+1 )\)th observations.

Why median? (Hinglish): Example from source: office mein 4 workers ki salaries 5000, 6500, 7500, 8000 aur supervisor ki 20000. Mean = (5000+6500+7500+8000+20000)/5 = 47000/5 = 9400 — par 4 mein se 4 log is se bahut kam kama rahe hain! Mean extreme values se distort ho jaata hai. Median isliye use karte hain — wo extreme values se affect nahi hota.
Example 3 — Median of Ungrouped Data SOURCE

Problem: Marks (out of 15) of 35 students (frequency table in source). Find median.

With frequencies, cumulative frequency hai 18th observation ki value = 7.
Median = value of \(\frac{n+1}{2} = \frac{35+1}{2} = 18\)th observation.
Final Answer: Median = 7 marks.

Advantages

  • Easy to understand and calculate; works even with open-end classes / unequal widths.
  • Applicable to qualitative data where ranking is possible.

Disadvantages

  • Data must be arranged in order.
  • Cannot be treated algebraically (composite median impossible).
  • Unsuitable when large/small values need weightage; interpolation assumes uniform spread in median class.
  • More affected by sampling fluctuations than A.M.

4. Mode

Formal Definition — Mode

Mode is the observation that occurs most frequently in the data, i.e., the observation with maximum frequency. A distribution with one/two/more modes is unimodal/bimodal/multimodal; if all values have equal frequency, no mode exists.

Example 4 — Mode (Raw Data) SOURCE

Problem: Goals scored by a football team in 12 matches: 1, 2, 2, 3, 1, 2, 2, 4, 5, 3, 3, 4.

Frequency of 2 is 4 — maximum.
Final Answer: Modal score = 2.
Example 5 — Two Modes SOURCE

Problem: Data: 9, 6, 8, 9, 10, 7, 12, 15, 22, 15.

Arrange: 6,7,8,9,9,10,12,15,15,22.
Both 9 and 15 have frequency 2 (maximum).
Final Answer: Modes = 9 and 15 (bimodal).

Advantages

  • Often obtained by mere inspection; unaffected by extreme values.
  • Can be found for open-end classes (with equal-width classes and gradual concentration).

Disadvantages

  • Meaningless without large data; may not exist or may be non-unique.
  • Difficult from grouped data; cannot be treated algebraically.

Unit 1 · 1.3 Combined Mean & Weighted Mean

Lecture 1.3 + 1.1.1–1.1.3 SOURCE CONTENT

1. Combined Mean (Composite Mean)

Formal Definition — Combined Mean

When two or more groups have different means and different numbers of observations, the mean of the combined group is called the Combined Mean (also Composite Mean). For two groups: \(\bar{X}_c = \frac{n_1\bar{X}_1 + n_2\bar{X}_2}{n_1 + n_2}\).

Hinglish Explanation: Combined mean basically groups ko merge karke overall average nikalta hai. Har group ka mean uski size se weight hota hai — bada group zyada influence karta hai. Steps: (1) har mean ko uski group size se multiply karo, (2) products add karo, (3) total observations add karo, (4) divide. Note: combined mean hamesha individual means ke beech mein aata hai.

Procedure (Method)

  1. Multiply each mean by its group size \(n_i\bar{X}_i\)
  2. Add all products \(\sum n_i\bar{X}_i\)
  3. Add total observations \(\sum n_i\)
  4. Divide total product by total observations
Example 1 — Two Sections SOURCE

Problem: Average marks of 40 students in Section A = 65; 35 students in Section B = 70. Find combined mean.

Given: \(n_1=40, \bar{X}_1=65, n_2=35, \bar{X}_2=70\)
Numerator: \(40 \times 65 + 35 \times 70 = 2600 + 2450 = 5050\)
Denominator: \(40 + 35 = 75\)
Final Answer: \(\bar{X}_c = 5050/75 = 67.33\)
Example 2 — Salaries SOURCE

Problem: Average salary of 25 male employees ₹40,000; of 15 female employees ₹35,000. Combined average?

Numerator: \(25 \times 40000 + 15 \times 35000 = 1000000 + 525000 = 1525000\)
Denominator: 40
Final Answer: Combined average salary = ₹1525000/40 = ₹38,125.

2. Weighted Mean

Formal Definition — Weighted Mean

When different observations are assigned different levels of importance (weights), the average obtained is called the Weighted Mean: \(\bar{X}_w = \frac{\sum w_i x_i}{\sum w_i}\).

HinglishExplanation: Kabhi kabhi har value equal important nahi hoti — jaise consumer har saal alag quantity kharidta hai. Toh har value ko uske weight (quantity) se multiply karke divide karte hain. Probability mein weights = probabilities, aur weighted mean = expected value ke barabar hota hai — ye connection exams ke liye important hai.
  1. Multiply each value by its weight
  2. Add all weighted values \(\sum w_i x_i\)
  3. Add all weights \(\sum w_i\)
  4. Divide weighted total by weight total
Example 3 — Commodity Prices (Weighted vs Simple) SOURCE

Problem: A consumer buys a commodity @ ₹4.80, ₹6, ₹8, ₹12, ₹24 per unit in five successive years. Find average cost per unit if he buys (a) 1000 units each year (b) 1000, 800, 600, 400, 200 units respectively.

(a) Equal weights: \(\bar{x} = (4.80+6+8+12+24)/5 = 54.80/5 = ₹10.96\)
(b) Weights 1000,800,600,400,200 → weighted value = \(4.8(1000)+6(800)+8(600)+12(400)+24(200) = 4800+4800+4800+4800+4800 = 24000\); total weight = 3000
Weighted mean = 24000/3000 = ₹8.00
Final Answer: (a) ₹10.96, (b) ₹8.00 per unit. Weight diversion se mean kaise badalta hai — ye weighted mean ka point hai.

Exam Tip: Jab quantities/weights different hain, weighted mean simple mean se different aayega — exam exactly yahi test karta hai.

3. Connection to Probability (Lecture 1.1.1–1.1.3)

  • Combined mean is a linear combination of individual means scaled by frequencies.
  • If weights sum to 1, weighted mean = expected value \(E(X)\) of a discrete random variable.
  • Missing mean/frequency problems: rearrange the combined-mean formula algebraically.

Unit 1 · 1.4 Measures of Dispersion — Range & Quartile Deviation

Lecture 1.4 + NPTEL Lecture 04 SOURCE CONTENT

1. Concept of Dispersion (Hinglish)

Concept: Sirf averages dataset nahi describe karte. Example from source: do paddy varieties ka mean yield dono ka 42 kg — par ek consistent, doosri variable. Consistency ko measure karne ke liye dispersion chahiye — matlab observations average se kitne "scattered" hain. Measures: Range, Quartile Deviation, Mean Deviation, Standard Deviation. Ek achhi measure of dispersion rigidly defined ho, all items pe based ho, extreme items se unduly affect na ho, algebraic treatment allow kare, aur simple ho.

2. Range

Formal Definition — Range

Range is the simplest measure of dispersion, defined as the difference between the largest and the smallest values of the variable: \(R = L - S\) where L = largest value, S = smallest value.

In continuous series, two methods: (1) L = upper boundary of highest class, S = lower boundary of lowest class; (2) L = mid-value of highest class, S = mid-value of lowest class.

Merits

  • Simple to understand; easy to calculate.
  • Widely used in quality control, weather forecasts, share-price analysis.

Demerits

  • Very much affected by extreme items; based on only two observations.
  • Cannot be calculated from open-end classes; not suitable for mathematical treatment; rarely used.
Example 1 — Range & Percentage Change SOURCE

Problem: Weights of 11 forty-year-old men: 148, 154, 158, 160, 161, 162, 166, 170, 180, 195, 236 pounds. If the heaviest man is omitted, what is the percentage change in the range?

Original range: 236 − 148 = 168
After omitting 236: new range = 195 − 148 = 47
Percentage change = (168−47)/168 × 100 = 121/168 × 100 ≈ 72.02% decrease
Final Answer: Range decreases by ≈ 72% — showing how sensitive range is to extreme values.

Exam Tip: Ye question range ki extreme-value sensitivity ko test karta hai — classic concept-understanding check.

3. Quartile Deviation (Semi-Interquartile Range)

Formal Definition — Quartile Deviation

Quartile Deviation is an absolute measure of dispersion based on quartiles, also known as the semi-interquartile range: \(Q.D. = \frac{Q_3 - Q_1}{2}\), where interquartile range \(= Q_3 - Q_1\).

Individual series: \(Q_1\) = value of \(( \frac{n+1}{4} )\)th item; \(Q_3\) = value of \(( \frac{3(n+1)}{4} )\)th item.
Continuous series: \(Q_1 = L + ( \frac{\frac{n}{4} - m}{f} )C\), \(Q_3 = L_1 + ( \frac{\frac{3n}{4} - m_1}{f_1} )C\), where L = lower limit of quartile class, m = preceding cumulative frequency, f = quartile-class frequency, C = class length.
Example 2 — Quartiles of Raw Data SOURCE

Problem: Find upper/lower quartiles and quartile deviation of: 36, 43, 47, 28, 18, 9, 32.

Arrange: 9, 18, 28, 32, 36, 43, 47 (n = 7)
Q1 = value of (7+1)/4 = 2nd item = 18; Q3 = value of 3·(7+1)/4 = 6th item = 43
Q.D. = (43 − 18)/2 = 12.5
Final Answer: Q1 = 18, Q3 = 43, Q.D. = 12.5
Example 3 — QD of 9 Values SOURCE

Problem: Quartile deviation of: 5, 7, 8, 12, 15, 18, 21, 24, 30.

n = 9. Q1 = (9+1)/4 = 2.5th item → interpolate between 7 and 8 = 7.5; Q3 = 3×2.5 = 7.5th item → between 21 and 24 = 22.5
Q.D. = (22.5 − 7.5)/2 = 7.5
Final Answer: Q.D. = 7.5

Advantages / Limitations / Applications

Advantages

  • Simple to calculate; not affected by extreme values.
  • Useful for skewed distributions; suitable for open-end distributions.

Limitations

  • Ignores 50% of observations; less accurate than SD.
  • Not suitable for algebraic treatment.

Applications: Income distribution analysis, business statistics, educational performance analysis, economic studies, financial volatility (middle 50% of returns).

Unit 1 · 1.5 Mean Deviation

Lecture 1.5 SOURCE CONTENT

1. Formal Definition & Formula

Formal Definition — Mean Deviation

Mean Deviation of a set of observations is the arithmetic mean of the absolute deviations from the mean (or any other specified value A). Simple series: \(M.D. = \frac{1}{n}\sum |x_i - \bar{x}|\); frequency distribution: \(M.D. = \frac{1}{N}\sum f_i |x_i - \bar{x}|\). 'Mean Deviation' by itself usually refers to M.D. about the mean.

Hinglish Explanation: Deviations (xᵢ − A) positive ya negative ho sakte hain, isliye hum absolute values |xᵢ − A| lete hain, unka sum nikalte hain, aur fir unka arithmetic mean — yahi Mean Deviation hai. Usually mean ke about compute karte hain, par median ke about bhi liya ja sakta hai (mixed-concept question!).
Example 1 — MD about Median SOURCE

Problem: Calculate mean deviation about the median: 8, 15, 53, 49, 19, 62, 7, 15, 95, 77.

Arrange: 7, 8, 15, 15, (19, 49), 53, 62, 77, 95 → n = 10 (even) → Median = (19+49)/2 = 34
Compute |x − median| for each: 26, 19, 19, 15, 15, 28, 27, 19, 61, 43
Sum = 272 → M.D. = 272/10 = 27.2
Final Answer: Mean Deviation about median = 27.2

Exam Tip: Mixed concept — pehle median, phir deviation table. Table banao — marks table ke liye milte hain.

Unit 1 · Standard Deviation, Variance & Coefficient of Variation

Lecture 1.5 + 1.7 + slide deck SOURCE CONTENT

1. Standard Deviation

Formal Definition — Standard Deviation

Standard Deviation is the positive square-root of the arithmetic mean of the squares of the deviations of the given observations from their arithmetic mean. Denoted by s (sample) or σ (population): \(s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2}\) (raw), \(s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum f_i(x_i-\bar{x})^2}\) (grouped).

Hinglish Explanation: SD basically deviations ka square karke unka mean leta hai, fir uska positive square root. Squaring ka matlab: positive/negative cancel nahi hote, aur extreme values ko zyada weight milta hai. SD poore observations se based hai isliye sabse important measure of dispersion hai.

Variance & Coefficient of Variation

Formal Definition — Variance

The square of the standard deviation is called variance: variance = (SD)².

Formal Definition — Coefficient of Variation

The Coefficient of Variation (C.V.) is a relative measure of dispersion obtained by dividing the standard deviation by the mean and expressing it as a percentage: \(C.V. = \frac{\sigma}{\bar{x}} \times 100\). Higher C.V. → more variable/less stable; lower C.V. → more consistent.

Why CV? SD units mein hota hai (cm, kg etc.), isliye alag units wali series compare karne ke liye CV (relative measure, %) use karte hain. Jiska CV zyada → wo series less consistent. Exam favorite!

Merits & Demerits of SD

Merits

  • Rigidly defined; definite value; based on all observations with actual signs.
  • Most important & widely used; amenable to further algebraic treatment.
  • Stable under sampling fluctuations; basis of correlation & sampling theory.

Demerits

  • Not easy to understand; difficult to calculate.
  • More weight to extreme values (squaring).
  • Absolute measure — cannot compare different-unit series (use C.V. instead).

2. Population vs Sample (Slide Deck)

Hinglish: Population variance mein denominator N hota hai (poori group), sample variance mein N−1 (bias correct karne ke liye, unbiased estimator). Formula: σ² = ∑(x−μ)²/N; s² = ∑(x−x̄)²/(N−1). Exam mein pehle decide karo — population ya sample.

3. Combined Variance (Slide Deck)

Combined Variance = \(\frac{\sum n_i(\sigma_i^2 + d_i^2)}{N}\), where \(d_i = \bar{X}_i - \bar{X}_c\) (group mean − combined mean). Includes within-group variance + between-group spread.

4. Common Pitfalls to Avoid

  • Forgetting to square deviations before summing.
  • Using N instead of N−1 for sample variance.
  • Incorrect mean before deviations.
  • Confusing SD with variance.
  • Rounding intermediate values too early.

Unit 2 · Moments

Lecture 2.1 SOURCE CONTENT

1. Introduction (Hinglish)

Concept: Moments distribution ki characteristics describe karte hain — tendency (mean), variation (variance), skewness aur kurtosis, sab moments se derive hote hain. "Moment" word mechanics se aaya hai. Statistics mein moments = deviations ki powers (1st, 2nd, 3rd …) ka arithmetic mean, deviations ya toh mean se ya arbitrary point A se liye jaate hain. Four moments (μ₁..μ₄) usually calculate kiye jaate hain. Raw moments = arbitrary point A ke about; agar A=0 → moments about origin; agar A = mean → central moments. μ₀ = 1, μ₁(mean) = 0, μ₂(mean) = variance σ².
Raw moment about arbitrary point A (grouped): \(\mu^{\prime}_r = \frac{1}{N}\sum f_i(x_i-A)^r\); ungrouped: \(\frac{1}{n}\sum(x_i-A)^r\)
Short-cut (change of scale): if \(d_i = (x_i - A)/h\), then \(\mu^{\prime}_r = h^r \cdot \frac{\sum f_i d_i^r}{N}\)
Moment about origin (A = 0): \(\mu^{\prime}_r = \frac{1}{N}\sum f_i x_i^r\); first origin moment = mean x̄
Central moment (about mean): \(\mu_r = \frac{1}{N}\sum f_i (x_i - \bar{x})^r\)

2. Key Results (Exam must-know)

  • \(\mu_0 = 1\), \(\mu_1 = 0\), \(\mu_2 = \sigma^2\) — "found very important in statistical theory and practice."
  • First raw moment about origin = mean; first central moment = 0.
  • Second raw & central moments = mean-square deviation & variance respectively.
  • Third & fourth moments feed skewness and kurtosis.
  • Change of origin does NOT affect central moments; change of scale multiplies r-th moment by \(h^r\).

Unit 2 · Moment Relations & Pearson β, γ Coefficients

Lecture 2.2 SOURCE CONTENT

1. Relation Between Central & Arbitrary-Point Moments

Hinglish: Jab actual mean fraction mein aata hai, hum deviations arbitrary point A se lete hain (raw moments), aur phir central moments (mean ke about) mein convert karte hain. Derivation mein xᵢ − x̄ = (xᵢ − A) − (x̄ − A) likh ke binomial expansion karte hain, aur μ′₁ = x̄ − A milta hai. Conversion formulas neeche hain — ye exam ke liye sabse important derivation hai.
\(\mu_2 = \mu^{\prime}_2 - {\mu^{\prime}}^2_1\)
\(\mu_3 = \mu^{\prime}_3 - 3\mu^{\prime}_1\mu^{\prime}_2 + 2{\mu^{\prime}}^3_1\)
\(\mu_4 = \mu^{\prime}_4 - 4\mu^{\prime}_1\mu^{\prime}_3 + 6{\mu^{\prime}}^2_1\mu^{\prime}_2 - 3{\mu^{\prime}}^4_1\)

2. Effect of Change of Origin & Scale

If \(u_i = (x_i - A)/h\): then \(\mu^{\prime}_r(x) = h^r \mu^{\prime}_r(u)\) (arbitrary point) and \(\mu_r(x) = h^r \mu_r(u)\) (central). Origin change affects nothing in central moments; scale multiplies by hʳ.

3. Sheppard's Corrections for Grouped Data

Grouping assumes frequencies uniformly distributed about midpoints — valid for symmetric data, but for highly skewed data or intervals > 1/20th of range, W. F. Sheppard's corrections apply (frequency tapers to zero both sides, h = class width):

\(\mu_2^{corrected} = \mu_2 - \frac{h^2}{12}\) \(\mu_4^{corrected} = \mu_4 - \frac{h^2}{12}\mu_2 + \frac{7h^4}{240}\) (μ₃ unchanged)

4. Pearson's Beta & Gamma Coefficients

\(\beta_1 = \frac{\mu_3^2}{\mu_2^3}\) (skewness) \(\gamma_1 = \pm\sqrt{\beta_1} = \frac{\mu_3}{\sigma^{3}}\) (sign from μ₃)
\(\beta_2 = \frac{\mu_4}{\mu_2^2}\) (kurtosis) \(\gamma_2 = \beta_2 - 3\)
Hinglish: β₁ hamesha positive aata hai kyunki μ₃ square ho jaata hai — direction (positive/negative skew) nahi batata. Isliye γ₁ = √β₁ use karte hain jiska sign μ₃ se aata hai. Kurtosis ke liye β₂ = μ₄/μ₂², aur γ₂ = β₂ − 3 (normal curve ke 3 se compare karne ke liye).

Unit 2 · Skewness — Karl Pearson & Bowley Methods

Lecture 2.3 + 1.3.2 SOURCE CONTENT

1. Concept (Hinglish + Definition)

Formal Definition — Skewness

Skewness means lack of symmetry. A distribution is symmetric if mean, median and mode coincide; otherwise asymmetric. If the right tail is longer → positively skewed (mean > median > mode); if the left tail is longer → negatively skewed (mean < median < mode).

Hinglish: Symmetric matlab mirror-image — center se dono sides same. Skewed data mein mean tail ki taraf shift ho jaata hai. Positive skew: mean > median > mode (right tail long, e.g. income data). Negative skew: mean < median < mode. Variance amount batata hai, skewness direction — dono alag cheezein hain.

2. Good Measure of Skewness — 3 Criteria

  1. Unit-free number (comparable across units)
  2. Zero for symmetric; ± sign shows direction
  3. Value moves from extreme negative to extreme positive smoothly

Absolute Measures (never use for comparison)

Sₖ = Mean − Median · Sₖ = Mean − Mode · Sₖ = (Q₃ − Q₂) − (Q₂ − Q₁)
Hinglish: Absolute measures same skewness ko chhote/bade variation wali distribution mein different meaning dete hain — isliye comparison ke liye relative measures (coefficients) use hote hain, jo pure numbers hote hain.

3. Karl Pearson's Coefficient of Skewness

\(S_k = \frac{Mean - Mode}{\sigma}\). If mode not well-defined: use Mode = 3·Median − 2·Mean → \(S_k = \frac{3(Mean - Median)}{\sigma}\). Zero = symmetric; positive if mean > mode. Usually between ±1 for moderately skewed; −3 < Sₖ < 3.

4. Bowley's Coefficient of Skewness (quartile-based)

\(S_k = \frac{(Q_3 - Q_2) - (Q_2 - Q_1)}{Q_3 - Q_1} = \frac{Q_3 + Q_1 - 2Q_2}{Q_3 - Q_1}\). Value between +1 and −1.
Example 1 — Pearson Reverse Problem SOURCE

Problem: Pearson's coefficient of skewness Sₖ = 0.64, σ = 13, Mean = 59.2. Find mode and median.

Sₖ = (Mean − Mode)/σ → Mode = Mean − Sₖ·σ = 59.2 − 0.64×13 = 59.2 − 8.32 = 50.88
Mode = 3·Median − 2·Mean → Median = (Mode + 2·Mean)/3 = (50.88 + 118.4)/3 = 169.28/3 ≈ 56.43
Final Answer: Mode ≈ 50.88, Median ≈ 56.43

5. Interpreting Skewness Values (Slide Deck)

  • 0 → perfectly symmetric; ±0.5 range → approximately symmetric
  • |value| > 0.5 → moderate; |value| > 1 → highly skewed
  • Magnitude = degree of asymmetry; sign = direction

Unit 2 · Kurtosis & Nature of Distribution

Lecture 2.4 + slide deck SOURCE CONTENT

1. Concept

Formal Definition — Kurtosis

Kurtosis gives a measure of the flatness/peakedness ("convexity", per Karl Pearson) of a distribution, relative to the normal curve. Measured by \(\beta_2 = \mu_4/\mu_2^2\); curves more peaked than normal are leptokurtic (β₂>3), flatter are platykurtic (β₂<3), and the normal curve is mesokurtic (β₂=3). Excess kurtosis γ₂ = β₂ − 3.

Hinglish: Central tendency + dispersion + skewness milke bhi distribution ka complete idea nahi dete — humein shape (peakedness/tailedness) bhi chahiye. Wahi kurtosis hai. High kurtosis = heavy tails + sharp peak (outliers zyada), low kurtosis = light tails + flat peak. Note: kurtosis ≠ skewness — asymmetry nahi measure karta.

2. Classification

Example 1 — Skewness & Kurtosis from Moments SOURCE

Problem (Lecture 2.4, Example 2): First four central moments: 0, 2.5, 0.7, 18.75. Find skewness and kurtosis.

Skewness β₁ = μ₃²/μ₂³ = (0.7)²/(2.5)³ = 0.49/15.625 = 0.031
Kurtosis β₂ = μ₄/μ₂² = 18.75/(2.5)² = 18.75/6.25 = 3
Final Answer: β₁ = 0.031 (nearly symmetric); β₂ = 3 → curve is mesokurtic.
Example 2 — Numeric Kurtosis SOURCE

Problem (slide deck): μ₄ = 128, σ = 2. Kurtosis?

β₂ = μ₄/σ⁴ = 128/2⁴ = 128/16 = 8
Excess = 8 − 3 = 5 > 0
Final Answer: β₂ = 8, excess = 5 → leptokurtic.

3. Common Pitfalls in Kurtosis (Slide Deck)

  • Do not confuse kurtosis with skewness (asymmetry).
  • Kurtosis does not measure the height of the peak alone — flat peak can still have high kurtosis if tails are heavy.
  • Sample kurtosis unstable with small samples.
  • Check outliers before computing kurtosis on raw data.
  • Misreading excess kurtosis (β₂−3) as raw kurtosis — a common error.

Unit 3 · Basics of Probability — Definitions and Laws

Lecture 3.1 SOURCE CONTENT

1. Core Definitions (Exam-ready)

Random Experiment

A random experiment is an experiment whose outcome cannot be predicted with certainty in advance (e.g. tossing a coin, rolling a die, drawing a card).

Outcome & Sample Space

An outcome is a possible result of a random experiment. The set of all possible outcomes is the sample space S (e.g. coin → S={H,T}; die → S={1,…,6}; two coins → S={HH,HT,TH,TT}).

Event

An event is a subset of the sample space, denoted by capital letters A, B, C (e.g. A = even number = {2,4,6}).

Types of Events

Simple/elementary: one outcome {2}. Compound: more than one outcome. Impossible: P(A)=0 (e.g. 7 on a die). Sure: P(S)=1. Complementary: A′ = S − A (e.g. A={2,4,6} → A′={1,3,5}).

Classical Definition of Probability

If an experiment has n equally likely outcomes and m favorable outcomes for event A, then \(P(A) = \frac{m}{n}\), with 0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1.

Hinglish: Probability basically uncertainty ko 0 se 1 ke beech number mein measure karta hai — 0 = impossible, 1 = sure. Classical definition ka logic simple hai: favorable/total. Applications: weather forecasting, insurance/risk, medical diagnosis, ML/AI, engineering reliability.

2. Laws of Probability

(A) Addition Law: \(P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)\). Mutually exclusive (can't occur together): P(A∩B)=0 → P(A∪B) = P(A)+P(B).
(B) Complement Law: \(P(A') = 1 - P(A)\).
(C) Multiplication Law: \(P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B|A)\). Independent events: \(P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B)\).

1. Conditional Probability

Formal Definition

Conditional Probability is the probability of occurrence of event A given that event B has already occurred: \(P(A|B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}\), where P(B) ≠ 0.

Hinglish: Jab ek event B already ho chuka ho, toh sample space effectively B ban jaata hai — us reduced space mein A ka probability. Example: deck se face card aaya — ab king honay ka chance sirf face cards (12) mein dekhenge.

2. Independent vs Dependent Events

Definitions

Independent events: occurrence of one does not affect the other (e.g. tossing two coins) — condition P(A∩B) = P(A)·P(B). Dependent events: occurrence of one affects the other (e.g. drawing cards without replacement).

3. Bayes' Theorem

Formal Statement

Bayes' Theorem is used to revise probabilities based on new information: \(P(A|B) = \frac{P(B|A)\,P(A)}{P(B)}\).

Applications: Medical testing, spam filtering, machine learning, decision-making.

1. Random Variable

Formal Definition

A random variable is a real-valued function whose domain is the set of possible outcomes of a random experiment and whose range is a subset of real numbers, satisfying: (I) each value can be assigned some probability; (II) the sum of all probabilities = 1. Notation: capital letters X, Y, Z; r.v. for random variable.

Hinglish: Random experiment ke har outcome se ek numerical characteristic attach karte hain — jaise die do baar phenka, X = odd numbers kitni baar aaye (0,1,2). Ab har value pe probability assign ho sakti hai. Discrete RV = countable values (students present); Continuous RV = interval ke saare real values (temperature, race timings).

For a continuous r.v. X with function f(x): \(f(x) = \lim_{dx\rightarrow 0} \frac{P[x \le X \le x+dx]}{dx}\), satisfying f(x) ≥ 0 and \(\int_R f(x)\,dx = 1\). The probability over an interval: \(P[a < X < b] = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\). A continuous r.v. has zero probability of taking any exact single value.

CDF

Unit 3 · Binomial Distribution

Lecture 3.4 SOURCE CONTENT

1. Concept

Formal Definition

The Binomial Distribution (Bernoulli distribution, after James Bernoulli) is a discrete probability distribution expressing the probability of one set of dichotomous alternatives (success/failure) in n independent trials: \(P(r) = {^nC_r}\,p^r q^{n-r} = \frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}p^r q^{n-r}\), r = 0,1,…,n. It is applicable when each trial has two outcomes, p stays constant, and trials are independent.

Hinglish: Binomial tab lagta hai jab har trial sirf success/failure deta hai (defective/OK, head/tail), p har baar same rahe (coin fair), aur trials independent hon (with replacement ya infinite population). Expansion (q+p)^n ke successive terms se P(0),P(1)… milte hain. Factorials: nCr = n!/(r!(n−r)!); 0! = 1.

Assumptions (must list in exam)

  1. Each trial has only two possible outcomes (success/failure).
  2. The probability p remains the same every trial.
  3. Trials are statistically independent.
  4. Number of trials n is known and finite.

2. Mean & Standard Deviation

Mean µ = np · SD σ = √(npq), q = 1 − p
Example 1 — Four or More Heads SOURCE

p = q = 1/2, n = 6 → P(r) = ⁶C_r(1/2)^6
P(4) = 15/64, P(5) = 6/64, P(6) = 1/64
P(≥4) = (15+6+1)/64 = 22/64 = 11/32
Final Answer: 11/32 ≈ 0.3438
Example 2 — Mean & SD of Defective Bolts SOURCE

Problem (Illustration 3): p = 0.1 defective; n = 500 bolts. Mean & SD?

µ = np = 500 × 0.1 = 50 defective expected
σ = √(npq) = √(500·0.1·0.9) = √45 ≈ 6.71
Final Answer: Mean 50, SD ≈ 6.71

3. Fitting a Binomial Distribution

  1. Determine p, q (p = 1−q). If p=q then symmetric; p<0.5 → positively skewed.
  2. Expand (p+q)ⁿ; n = one less than the number of terms.
  3. Multiply each term by total frequency N to get expected frequencies.

  • P(r successes) = ⁿC_r pʳ qⁿ⁻ʳ.

Unit 3 · Poisson Distribution

Lecture 3.5 SOURCE CONTENT

1. Concept

Formal Definition

The Poisson distribution (Simeon Poisson) counts the number of occurrences of an event in a fixed interval of time/space, used when events are infrequent. When n is large (n>50) and p is very small (even <0.01) so that np is constant, the binomial is approximated by Poisson: \(P(r) = \frac{m^r e^{-m}}{r!}\), r = 0,1,2,…, e ≈ 2.7183, m = np (mean).

Hinglish: Poisson = binomial ki limiting form jab success bahut rare ho (defects, accidents). Single parameter m se poori distribution banti hai. Kabhi bhi rare-event + large n + small p dekho, toh Poisson flag karo.

Characteristics

  • Discrete probability distribution; limiting form of binomial.
  • Range: 0 ≤ r < ∞; single parameter m.
  • Positively skewed; skewness decreases as m increases.
  • Mean = Variance = np = m; SD = √m.
Example 1 — 2% Defective Toys SOURCE

Problem (Illustration 5): 2% of toys defective. P(exactly 5 defectives in shipment of 200)? Mean and SD?

n large, p small → Poisson. m = np = 200×0.02 = 4
P(5) = m⁵e⁻ᵐ/5! = (1024×0.0183)/120 ≈ 0.156
Mean = 4, σ = √4 = 2
Final Answer: P(5) ≈ 0.156; mean 4; SD 2.
Example 2 — Binomial vs Poisson Comparison SOURCE

Problem (Illustration 6): 30 tools, p(defect) = 0.02. P(4 defective) via (i) binomial (ii) Poisson.

(i) Binomial: ³⁰C₄(0.02)⁴(0.98)²⁶ = 27405 × 1.6×10⁻⁷ × 0.59 ≈ 0.00259
(ii) Poisson: m = 0.6 → P(4) = (0.6)⁴e⁻⁰·⁶/4! = 0.1296×0.5488/24 ≈ 0.00296
Final Answer: 0.00259 vs 0.00296 — Poisson approximates binomial when p small.

2. Fitting a Poisson Distribution

  1. Obtain m = np from observed data.
  2. Compute P(r) = mʳe⁻ᵐ/r! for each r.
  3. Multiply each probability by N to get expected frequencies.

m = (0·214 + 1·92 + 2·20 + 3·3 + 4·1)/330 = 145/330 ≈ 0.439
P(r) = (0.439)ʳe⁻⁰·⁴³⁹/r!; expected frequency = 330 × P(r)

Unit 3 · Normal Distribution (Continuous)

Lecture 3.6 SOURCE CONTENT

1. Concept

Formal Definition

The Normal distribution is the most versatile continuous probability distribution, suitable for variables continuous in magnitude, fully determined by two parameters — mean µ and standard deviation σ. The standard random variable is \(Z = \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma}\).

Hinglish: Normal curve bell-shaped hai, symmetric (skewness = 0), mean = median = mode. Tails axis ko kabhi touch nahi karti. Z-score bataata hai X mean se kitne SD door hai — phir Z-table se area/probability nikalte hain. Do parameters yaad rakho: µ aur σ.

Characteristics (learn all)

  1. Single peak — unimodal, bell-shaped.
  2. Symmetric → mean = median = mode (skewness 0).
  3. Tails extend indefinitely, never touching the axis.
  4. Total area under curve = 1.

Unit 1 — Central Tendency

Mean (ungrouped): \(\bar{x} = \frac{\sum x_i}{n}\) · (grouped): \(\frac{\sum f_i x_i}{\sum f_i}\) — basic average; any data.
Short-cut: \(\bar{x} = A + \frac{\sum f_iD_i}{\sum f_i}, D_i = x_i - A\) — big numbers simplified.
Step-deviation: \(\bar{x} = A + \frac{\sum f_iu_i}{\sum f_i}h,\ u_i = \frac{x_i-A}{h}\) — equal class width h.
Combined mean: \(\bar{X}_c = \frac{n_1\bar{X}_1 + n_2\bar{X}_2}{n_1+n_2}\) — merging groups.
Weighted mean: \(\bar{X}_w = \frac{\sum w_i x_i}{\sum w_i}\) — importance weights.
Median: value of (n+1)/2-th (odd n); average of n/2 & (n/2+1)-th (even n) after ordering.
Mode: maximum-frequency value; empirical: Mode = 3·Median − 2·Mean.

Unit 1 — Dispersion

Range = L − S (largest − smallest).
Quartile Deviation = (Q₃ − Q₁)/2; interquartile range = Q₃ − Q₁. Quartiles (individual): (n+1)/4-th, 3(n+1)/4-th items. Grouped: \(Q_1 = L + \frac{n/4 - m}{f}C\).
Mean Deviation = \(\frac{1}{n}\sum|x_i - \bar{x}|\) / \(\frac{1}{N}\sum f_i|x_i - \bar{x}|\).
Standard Deviation: \(s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum(x_i - \bar{x})^2}\); grouped: \(\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum f_i(x_i - \bar{x})^2}\); step form: \(C\sqrt{\frac{\sum fd^2}{N} - ( \frac{\sum fd}{N} )^2}\).
Variance = (SD)²; Population σ² = Σ(x−µ)²/N; Sample s² = Σ(x−x̄)²/(n−1).
Coefficient of Variation: C.V. = \(\frac{\sigma}{\bar{x}} \times 100\%\).
Combined Variance = \(\frac{\sum n_i(\sigma_i^2 + d_i^2)}{N},\ d_i = \bar{X}_i - \bar{X}_c\).

Unit 2 — Moments, Skewness, Kurtosis

Raw moment about A: \(\mu^{\prime}_r = \frac{\sum f_i(x_i - A)^r}{N}\); with short-cut: \(h^r \frac{\sum f_i d_i^r}{N},\ d_i = (x_i - A)/h\).
